# UKRN-S design commitments: a plain-language argument

*Written to be read standalone by a UKRN-S board member, a funder, or a
member-institution lead with no prior engagement with the framework. No
jargon required beyond the terms defined here.*

## What UKRN-S inherited, and what it didn't

When the Open Research Programme wound down, UKRN-S — a small community
interest company — inherited a lot: a working training programme that taught
2,816 researchers across 24 institutions, an evaluation instrument with
research-ethics approval, a detailed model of how research institutions take
up new practices, and a set of design patterns distilled from four years of
trying.

But it inherited four honest gaps. It does not know whether the people it
trained actually changed how they work — attendance was counted, behaviour
was not. It cannot yet compare what worked at one institution with what
worked at another while the programme is running, only afterwards. The
institutions themselves reported their numbers but were never asked to
evaluate their own local situations. And the design decisions UKRN-S now has
to make, on a calendar, rest on recommendations that were never checked
against evidence.

## What we recommend, in plain terms

**Run the team as five named roles, not as goodwill.** A Training Manager who
owns delivery; an Evaluator who owns analysis; a Convener who owns the
sector-level conversation; a Researcher-Advisor who owns the evidence base;
and Institutional Leads who own their own institutions. Each role gets
explicit time, explicit data flows between them, and explicit rules for who
decides what. This is the single most important commitment, and it is the
cheapest: it costs management attention, not money.

**Hire an evaluator before anything else.** The predecessor programme had
data and tools but, at the moments we observed, no one actually performing
analysis with them. Without this one appointment, none of the four gaps can
close — the tools stay on the shelf. Our simulation work says the evaluator
can be one central person or a distributed network of peers; distributed only
works if at least about half the institutions genuinely take part, so measure
that first.

**Do not marry a theory.** The working paper carried several attractive
frames — a "geometric synthesis", a sustainability triangle, an analogy to
how ant colonies work. We checked each against simulation runs. Two hold up
(Normalization Process Theory and the pattern-language approach) and stay.
One — the ant-colony-style simulation — is useful as a testing tool but says
nothing about what UKRN-S *is*. The rest we set aside: pleasant language,
sometimes, but nothing the company should depend on. The design works without
any of them.

**Watch the funders as a real constraint.** When research funders put money
in, every institution moves forward together on delivery, but they spread
apart on ambition — some leap ahead, some stall. That pattern was invisible
in the old framework. UKRN-S should treat funding cycles as a first-class
thing to monitor and plan around.

**Keep score honestly.** Every commissioned course has a named owner with
time. No evaluation result changes a service decision without the evaluator
having analysed it. No public commitment is made without its evidence written
down. Every change traces to a specific piece of evidence — and so does every
decision *not* to change.

## Why believe this

Every recommendation above is tied to named evidence: the delivery numbers,
the focus-group analysis, the ethics-approved evaluation instrument, and
simulation runs that stress-tested the design across the plausible ways the
organisation could be configured. Where the simulation surprised us —
evaluation *timing* matters less than we thought; funders matter more — we
said so and revised.

If the first real evaluation wave supports the design, UKRN-S has an
evidence-grounded basis for its commitments. If it doesn't, the gaps the wave
surfaces are exactly where the next round of work goes. Either outcome is a
success; only not asking would be a failure.
