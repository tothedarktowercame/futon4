* The nine steps
From a coupling someone hit, to a checked enactment. Each step names its
input, its output artefact, the tool that does it or =by hand=, and its check.

| Step | By hand | Check |
|------+---------+-------|
| 1 someone hits the coupling | yes | n/a |
| 2 measure it | no | re-run the enumeration |
| 3 choose the grain | yes | grain check |
| 4 write the cascade | yes | cascade check |
| 5 wire it | no | wiring check |
| 6 choose a target, record the click | yes | PROOF-2a clause checks |
| 7 predict | no | the prediction reproduces |
| 8 enact | yes | the wants' own checks; conformance |
| 9 observe, update belief | no | clause statuses |

* What the method claims
Four steps are judgements made by a person. The claim is not that judgement
can be automated away, but that a judgement must be *recorded in a form
something else can contradict*. Every step now has a check that can
contradict it.

* The grain step
The step most often got wrong, and the hardest to check. The grain of a
resolver is *what its lookup is keyed by*, which is visible in its argument
list: a function taking a role and answering which seat plays it is at the
role grain; one taking an agent id and answering which provider it is, is not.
A cascade declares the grain it chose; a check compares that against the
enacted resolver and against the file it lives in.
